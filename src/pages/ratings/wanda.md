Wanda repudiates the Hollywood dramatic formula in favor of a more realistic, gritty portrait of alienation and hopelessness. Vignettes of desperation and aimlessness depict her intricacies and plights as a drifting loner. At first, the cinéma vérité style of filming parallels the urban isolation she feels in the town, showing her wandering around while life goes on. Her downtrodden luck cascades into a stultifying affair, culminating in her losing money. The same style intimately places the viewer in close proximity to her, providing us insights into her emotional downturn. When she encounters Mr. Dennis, that same style pervades within the film an unfortunate intimacy. Even with his terrible treatment of her, it is a human connection that had been long eluding her. The road trip and robbery planning become a heart-rending reminder of her social starvation, as through it she reveals her idea of existence: one of endless meandering and meaninglessness. In a twisted sense, his entrusting of her occupies that incapacity, leading to the intertwining of their lives. In the end, his death shatters the one source of solace in Wanda's life. She goes back into a state of nihilistic existence, once again resigned to sexual objectification and hollow socialization ad nauseum.

I was reminded of James Benning in this film, as both directors capture different slivers of less explored Americana in film. Some of the shots — like the sequences of town life, the soot-covered coal hills, or the imposition of massive landscapes — seem to be contrived for maximum realism. Badlands also comes into mind with naturalistic imagery and landscape shots, although this could just be an ill-conceived connection.
